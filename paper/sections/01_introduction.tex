\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Reasoning language models such as \dsseven{} and \qweneight{}
\citep{deepseekr1,qwen3,openai_o1} solve hard problems by emitting long
chains of thought, often thousands of tokens per query. Much of this
computation is spent \emph{after} the model has effectively decided on an
answer, which makes \emph{early exit}---halting generation once the answer is
settled---an appealing lever for reducing inference cost
\citep{overthinking,dynasor_certaindex}.

A widely used family of early-exit methods works by \emph{probing}. At regular
intervals during generation, the partial trajectory is queried for its current
answer (e.g., by appending a short ``\texttt{Final Answer:}'' suffix), and the
decoder is halted once these probes \emph{agree}---the same answer appears in
several consecutive probes, or a share threshold over a recent window is
exceeded \citep{dynasor_certaindex}. The implicit assumption is that
\emph{intermediate consensus is a reliable signal of termination}: once the
model repeatedly says the answer is $x$, further reasoning is unlikely to
change it.

This paper asks whether that assumption holds, and whether any probe-based
stopping rule can exploit it \emph{safely}. We answer both in the negative, and
we do so under a preregistered protocol designed to make the negative result
credible rather than an artifact of a poorly chosen heuristic.

\paragraph{Finding 1: False consensus.}
On \textsc{MATH}500 with \dsseven{}, we log a dense probe stream for every
problem without ever intervening (Section~\ref{sec:falseconsensus}). Perfect
whole-trajectory consensus is nearly trustworthy ($98.9\%$ correct), but the
signal a stopping rule actually sees online---agreement over a recent
window---is not: a unanimous last-five-probe window is correct only $93.5\%$ of
the time, with $6.5\%$ \emph{false consensus}. Simulating a Dynasor-style stop
(three consecutive agreeing, certain, non-empty probes) fires on $416/500$
problems and commits to an answer that is correct only $69.2\%$ of the time,
whereas letting those same problems run to completion reaches $85.6\%$---a
$16.4\pp$ loss. The mechanism is recovery: of problems whose first probe is
wrong, $76.3\%$ eventually flip to correct. Intermediate consensus is simply
not terminal.

\paragraph{Finding 2: No safe-and-saving rule exists (preregistered).}
A natural response is that a smarter rule---higher maturity thresholds,
persistence requirements, certainty filters, adaptive probing---would recover
safety while keeping the savings. We test this exhaustively
(Sections~\ref{sec:method}--\ref{sec:results}). We freeze one reasoning
trajectory per problem, re-probe it offline so that no stopping schedule can
alter the underlying reasoning, and sweep a seven-dimensional rule schema of
$17{,}712$ candidate rules across two models and three benchmarks. Splits are
problem-grouped ($60/20/20$ train/dev/test) and the operating-point gates are
fixed in advance; the test split and two confirmation models are never visible
to selection. \emph{No rule clears the preregistered conservative gate.} The
safest rule in the entire space still costs $1.85\pp$ per model, every rule with
positive net token savings costs at least $4.87\pp$, and sophisticated
event-triggered (``adaptive'') probing is strictly worse than a simple window
share.

\paragraph{Finding 3: Two taxes, one intrinsic.}
We separate the cost of stopping into an \emph{accuracy tax}---you lose
accuracy purely because you commit before reasoning finishes, independent of
how you probe---and a \emph{probe tax}---the token cost of the probes
themselves, which turns the savings of conservative rules negative
(Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}). The accuracy tax is intrinsic and survives any
probing scheme; the probe tax is a function of probe density. This distinction
is what makes the negative result robust: the $1.85\pp$ floor is not an artifact
of dense probing.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item We define and quantify \textbf{false consensus} in reasoning models,
  including a calibration analysis showing window share systematically
  overstates reliability, and an error taxonomy of stopped-but-wrong cases
  (Section~\ref{sec:falseconsensus}).
  \item We introduce a \textbf{preregistered, probe-independent evaluation
  protocol} for early-exit rules---frozen trajectories, offline dense
  re-probing, a seven-dimensional rule schema, and fixed selection gates---and
  release the design and sweep artifacts (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
  \item We report a \textbf{near-impossibility result}: across $17{,}712$
  rules, no stop is simultaneously safe and token-saving, and we characterize
  the full accuracy--savings frontier (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
  \item We provide a \textbf{mechanism}: a direction-of-effect analysis and an
  accuracy-tax/probe-tax decomposition, and we compare against raw prior-work
  early-stoppers (Sections~\ref{sec:mechanism}--\ref{sec:baselines}).
\end{enumerate}
