\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Reasoning language models \citep{deepseekr1,qwen3,openai_o1} can spend thousands
of tokens on a single answer. \emph{Early exit}---halting a chain of thought once
its answer is settled---could cut that cost \citep{overthinking,dynasor_certaindex},
but only if the stopping signal is safe. A widely used signal is
\emph{intermediate answer consensus}: at regular intervals a partial trajectory
is probed for its current answer (e.g., by appending a short
``\texttt{Final Answer:}'' suffix) and generation is stopped once recent probes
\emph{agree} \citep{dynasor_certaindex}. The underlying assumption is simple: if
the model repeatedly says $x$, it has probably finished reasoning about $x$. This
paper asks whether that assumption is warranted: \textbf{is intermediate
agreement a safe signal on which to stop?}

Our answer is \textbf{no}, and the reason is not a badly tuned threshold.
Intermediate consensus\footnote{``False consensus'' here denotes non-terminal
intermediate probe agreement, unrelated to the social-psychology term
\citep{ross1977false}; we study answer-level, not token/layer-level, early exit
\citep{graves2016act,schuster2022confident}.} forms \emph{while the answer is
still moving}. On a frozen replay, a consensus stop destroys a correct-in-the-end
answer up to ${\sim}\mathbf{45}\times$ more often than it rescues a wrong one at an
aggressive latest-probe stop. Demanding more agreement helps only by stopping
less often: at the largest windows the ratio falls to ${\sim}2\times$, but the
rule fires on a fifth as many problems and saves almost nothing.

We make this hard to dismiss as ``you did not tune it well'' by searching the
space exhaustively under commitment: on frozen trajectories from two models and
three benchmarks we run a \emph{preregistered} sweep of the consensus signal---a
window size $\times$ share threshold family plus operational knobs, $3{,}520$
rules---with problem-grouped splits and acceptance gates fixed in advance
(Sections~\ref{sec:method}--\ref{sec:results}). The outcome does not depend on
any single fragile estimate: \textbf{not one of the $3{,}520$ rules is both safe
and saving---none clears any gate}, and even large windows only trade the drop
away for zero saving.

Why should agreement fail so consistently? Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} gives
three pieces of evidence. Agreement is informative in self-consistency because
the reasoning paths are sampled \emph{independently}; probes of one trajectory
share a prefix, so they cannot outvote each other's errors. Probing the same
frozen prefix with two different wordings returns different answers $57\%$ of the
time in the first tenth of a trajectory, against $11\%$ at the end---early
``answers'' are largely artifacts of being asked. And hand-labelling $134$ wrong
stops finds that three in five commit to an answer the model had not converged
on, against one in five that it had genuinely settled on.

To show this is the \emph{signal} and not early exit, we
sweep one non-consensus method---DEER, which reads the model's confidence at a
reasoning boundary---through the identical pipeline and gates: it clears all
three, losing as little as $0.3\pp$ while saving $28\%$. The consensus frontier
reproduces on a held-out test split ($r{=}0.98$) and on unseen models at
$4\times$ the scale and a different architecture (Section~\ref{sec:results}). Two
published-signal checks bracket the contrast: CertaIndex (CoT), reproduced from
its released implementation, loses $56$--$70\pp$ on the same trajectories, while
DEER clears the gates on the unseen models too. Figure~\ref{fig:idea} is the
whole paper in one picture. Our claim is about agreement used \emph{alone} as a
stopping criterion; combined with a signal that reflects the model's own estimate
it may still be useful, and we return to this in Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}.

\paragraph{Contribution.}
Our contribution is an explanation of \emph{why} intermediate answer-consensus
cannot serve as an early-exit signal on its own, together with the evidence that
no tuning escapes it. Three components support the claim:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{An account of what probe agreement can and cannot mean.}
  Self-consistency's guarantee comes from independently sampled paths; probes of a
  single trajectory share a prefix, so agreement supplies no vote that can outweigh
  an error, and a stop has nothing to protect it (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}).
  \item \textbf{Direct evidence of what the probes agree on.} Two annotators
  labelled all $134$ stopped-but-wrong cases ($\kappa{=}0.82$): three in five
  commit to an answer the model had not converged on, and only one in five to a
  settled one. Probing forces an output rather than reading a belief, which also
  explains why a longer window reduces the damage without removing it---and why it
  does so only by declining to stop (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}).
  \item \textbf{A preregistered search showing this is not a tuning failure}
  (frozen trajectories, offline re-probing, a two-parameter consensus family
  enumerated to $3{,}520$ rules, gates fixed in advance): \emph{no} consensus rule
  is both safe and token-saving; the frontier reproduces on a held-out split and
  two unseen models; and a non-consensus signal (DEER) swept under the identical
  protocol \emph{clears the same gates} on the same trajectories, localizing the
  failure to the signal (Sections~\ref{sec:method}--\ref{sec:results}). We release
  the protocol, trajectories, and sweep in full.
\end{itemize}
