\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Reasoning language models such as \dsseven{} and \qweneight{}
\citep{deepseekr1,qwen3,openai_o1} solve hard problems by emitting long
chains of thought, often thousands of tokens per query. Much of this
computation is spent \emph{after} the model has effectively decided on an
answer, which makes \emph{early exit}---halting generation once the answer is
settled---an appealing lever for reducing inference cost
\citep{overthinking,dynasor_certaindex}.\footnote{We use ``false consensus'' for
the phenomenon that intermediate probe agreement is non-terminal; the term is
unrelated to the social-psychology ``false consensus effect'' \citep{ross1977false}.
We also mean answer-level early exit (stopping a chain of thought), not
token- or layer-level early exit \citep{graves2016act,schuster2022confident}.}

A widely used family of early-exit methods works by \emph{probing}. At regular
intervals during generation, the partial trajectory is queried for its current
answer (e.g., by appending a short ``\texttt{Final Answer:}'' suffix), and the
decoder is halted once these probes \emph{agree}---the same answer appears in
several consecutive probes, or a share threshold over a recent window is
exceeded \citep{dynasor_certaindex}. The implicit assumption is that
\emph{intermediate consensus is a reliable signal of termination}: once the
model repeatedly says the answer is $x$, further reasoning is unlikely to
change it.

This paper asks whether that assumption holds, and whether any probe-based
stopping rule can exploit it \emph{safely}. We answer both in the negative for
the family of rules we search, and we do so under a preregistered protocol
designed to make the negative result credible rather than an artifact of a
poorly chosen heuristic.

\paragraph{Finding 1: False consensus.}
On \textsc{MATH}500 with \dsseven{}, we log a dense probe stream for every
problem without ever intervening (Section~\ref{sec:falseconsensus}). Perfect
whole-trajectory consensus is nearly trustworthy ($98.9\%$ correct), but the
signal a stopping rule actually sees online---agreement over a recent
window---is not: a unanimous last-five-probe window is correct only $93.5\%$ of
the time, with $6.5\%$ \emph{false consensus}. A naive stop that halts at the
first three consecutive agreeing, non-empty probes fires on $416/500$ problems
and commits to an answer that is correct only $69.2\%$ of the time, whereas
letting those same problems run to completion reaches $85.6\%$---a $16.4\pp$
loss. (This is a simplified simulation, not the Certaindex algorithm, which we
reproduce faithfully in Section~\ref{sec:baselines}.) The mechanism is recovery:
of problems whose first probe is wrong, $76.3\%$ eventually flip to correct.
Intermediate consensus is simply not terminal.

\paragraph{Finding 2: No safe-and-saving rule in the searched space (preregistered).}
A natural response is that a smarter rule---higher maturity thresholds,
persistence requirements, certainty filters, adaptive probing---would recover
safety while keeping the savings. We test this exhaustively over a large
preregistered space (Sections~\ref{sec:method}--\ref{sec:results}). We freeze
one reasoning trajectory per problem, re-probe it offline so that no stopping
schedule can alter the underlying reasoning, and sweep a seven-dimensional rule
schema of $17{,}712$ candidate rules across two models and three benchmarks.
Splits are problem-grouped ($60/20/20$ train/dev/test) and the operating-point
gates are fixed in advance; the test split and two confirmation models are never
visible to selection. \emph{On the development set, no rule clears the
preregistered conservative gate.} The safest rule in the entire space still
costs $1.85\pp$ per model; separately, under dense-probe accounting every rule
with positive net token savings costs at least $4.87\pp$, and sophisticated
event-triggered (``adaptive'') probing is dominated by a simple window share.

\paragraph{Finding 3: Two taxes, one intrinsic.}
We separate the cost of stopping into an \emph{accuracy tax}---you lose
accuracy purely because you commit before reasoning finishes, independent of
how you probe---and a \emph{probe tax}---the token cost of the probes
themselves, which turns the net savings of conservative rules negative under
dense probing (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}). The accuracy tax is intrinsic and
survives any probing scheme; the probe tax is a function of probe density. This
distinction is what keeps the negative result robust: the $1.85\pp$ accuracy
floor is not an artifact of dense probing.

\paragraph{Finding 4: The signal is the problem, not early exit.}
The failure is specific to \emph{consensus}. Reproducing prior early-stoppers on
the same frozen trajectories, a faithful Certaindex collapses by $56$--$70\pp$
(Section~\ref{sec:baselines}), but DEER \citep{deer}---which stops on the
model's confidence in a trial answer at reasoning boundaries, \emph{not} on
probe agreement---stays within $\sim\!1\pp$ of full accuracy. Pushed into an
online controller with a fast-commit path and a retained verification branch,
this boundary-confidence signal saves $\sim\!44\%$ of tokens at essentially
neutral accuracy ($+2.8\pp$ on one model; Section~\ref{sec:boundary}). Early
exit is not hopeless; consensus is simply the wrong signal.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item We define and quantify \textbf{false consensus} in reasoning models,
  including a calibration analysis showing window share systematically
  overstates reliability, and an error taxonomy of stopped-but-wrong cases
  (Section~\ref{sec:falseconsensus}).
  \item We introduce a \textbf{preregistered, probe-independent evaluation
  protocol} for early-exit rules---frozen trajectories, offline dense
  re-probing, a seven-dimensional rule schema, and fixed selection gates---and
  release the design and sweep artifacts (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
  \item We report that, across $17{,}712$ rules, \textbf{no stop in the searched
  space is simultaneously safe and token-saving} on the development set, and we
  characterize the full accuracy--savings frontier (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
  \item We provide a \textbf{mechanism} (an accuracy-tax/probe-tax
  decomposition) and reproduce prior early-stoppers on identical frozen
  trajectories, showing the consensus-based ones collapse
  (Sections~\ref{sec:mechanism}--\ref{sec:baselines}).
  \item We give \textbf{constructive evidence} that a different signal
  (boundary confidence with a verification branch) escapes the accuracy tax,
  saving $\sim\!44\%$ of tokens at neutral accuracy in an online deployment
  (Section~\ref{sec:boundary}).
\end{enumerate}
