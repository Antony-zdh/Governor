\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Reasoning language models \citep{deepseekr1,qwen3,openai_o1} can spend thousands
of tokens on a single answer. \emph{Early exit}---halting a chain of thought once
its answer is settled---could cut that cost \citep{overthinking,dynasor_certaindex},
but only if the stopping signal is safe. A widely used signal is
\emph{intermediate answer consensus}: at regular intervals a partial trajectory
is probed for its current answer (e.g., by appending a short
``\texttt{Final Answer:}'' suffix) and generation is stopped once recent probes
\emph{agree} \citep{dynasor_certaindex}. The underlying assumption is simple: if
the model repeatedly says $x$, it has probably finished reasoning about $x$, and
recent work reports it paying off---on several benchmarks answers ``converge''
after ${\sim}60\%$ of the reasoning, and stopping on consensus saves tokens with
little \emph{aggregate} accuracy loss \citep{liu2025answerconvergence,dynasor_certaindex}.
This paper asks whether that apparent success is \emph{safe}: \textbf{is
intermediate agreement a signal one can stop on without quietly losing accuracy?}

Our answer is \textbf{no}, and the reason is not a badly tuned threshold---it is
the signal itself: repeated intermediate agreement measures whether the current
answer is \emph{stable}, not whether the reasoning has \emph{terminated}, and the
two come apart---a \emph{stability--terminality gap}. This reconciles the apparent
success above: the aggregate savings are real, but they ride on a
\emph{directional} accuracy tax that safety-blind, probe-cost-free evaluation
averages away. Because continued reasoning corrects the answer far more often than
it breaks it, stopping on consensus\footnote{``False consensus'' here denotes
non-terminal intermediate probe agreement, unrelated to the social-psychology term
\citep{ross1977false}; we study answer-level, not token/layer-level, early exit
\citep{graves2016act,schuster2022confident}.} destroys a correct-in-the-end answer
up to $\mathbf{35}$ times more often at an aggressive stop
(Section~\ref{sec:mechanism})---an accuracy tax that is a property of the reasoning
trajectory, not of how one probes it, so no rule in the searched space escapes it. We make the claim hard to dismiss as
``you did not tune it well'' by searching the space exhaustively under
commitment: on frozen trajectories from two models and three benchmarks we run a
\emph{preregistered} sweep of the consensus signal---a window size $\times$ share
threshold family plus operational knobs, $3{,}520$ rules---with problem-grouped
splits and acceptance gates fixed in advance
(Sections~\ref{sec:method}--\ref{sec:results}). The outcome does not depend on
any single fragile estimate: \textbf{not one of the $3{,}520$ rules is both safe
and saving---none clears any gate}, and even large windows only trade the drop
away for zero saving. To show this is the \emph{signal} and not early exit, we
sweep one non-consensus method---DEER, which reads the model's confidence at a
reasoning boundary---through the identical pipeline and gates: it clears all
three, losing as little as $0.3\pp$ while saving $28\%$. The consensus frontier
reproduces on a held-out test split ($r{=}0.98$) and on unseen models at
$4\times$ the scale and a different architecture (Section~\ref{sec:results}). Two
published-signal checks bracket the contrast: a faithful Certaindex reproduction
on the same trajectories collapses by $56$--$70\pp$, while DEER clears the gates
on the unseen models too. Figure~\ref{fig:pareto} is the whole paper in one
picture: the
consensus rules that clear the gates in-sample on train leave the safe-and-saving
corner on dev and test, while the three DEER operating points selected on dev
stay in it out of sample.

\paragraph{Contribution.}
We give a rigorous, mechanism-backed demonstration---established so it cannot be
waved away as an untuned heuristic---that \emph{intermediate answer-consensus is
an unsafe early-exit signal for reasoning LLMs}, resting on three parts.
\textbf{(i) A preregistered, probe-independent test} (frozen trajectories, offline
re-probing, a two-parameter family of $3{,}520$ rules, gates fixed in advance),
released in full, under which \emph{no} consensus rule is both safe and saving
(Sections~\ref{sec:method}--\ref{sec:results}).
\textbf{(ii) A mechanism}: a directional ``continued-reasoning-corrects'' effect
($\gg\!1{:}1$) makes the accuracy cost intrinsic to the trajectory and independent
of probe density, so the failure is the \emph{signal}, not the particular rules we
searched (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}).
\textbf{(iii) Out-of-distribution and cross-signal confirmation}: the frontier
reproduces on a held-out split and two unseen models, a Certaindex reproduction
collapses, and a non-consensus signal (DEER) swept identically \emph{clears the
same gates}---localizing the failure to consensus and pointing to where a safe
signal must come from (Sections~\ref{sec:results}--\ref{sec:mechanism}).
