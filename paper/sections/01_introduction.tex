\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Reasoning language models \citep{deepseekr1,qwen3,openai_o1} can spend thousands
of tokens on a single answer. \emph{Early exit}---halting a chain of thought once
its reasoning is done---could cut that cost \citep{overthinking,dynasor_certaindex},
but it requires knowing \emph{when the reasoning has finished}, which the
trajectory does not announce. A natural and increasingly studied proxy is
\emph{intermediate answer consensus}: at regular intervals a partial trajectory
is probed for its current answer (e.g., by appending a short
``\texttt{Final Answer:}'' suffix), and generation is stopped once recent probes
\emph{agree} \citep{dynasor_certaindex}. The premise is that if the model
repeatedly says $x$, it has probably finished reasoning about $x$; recent work
reports answers that ``converge'' well before a trajectory ends and stops on that
convergence at little \emph{aggregate} accuracy cost
\citep{liu2025answerconvergence}.

We show that this proxy measures the wrong object. Under a fixed probing
procedure, repeated agreement establishes only that the current answer
\emph{persists}, not that the reasoning has \emph{terminated}, and the two come
apart---a \emph{stability--terminality gap}.\footnote{``False consensus'' here
denotes non-terminal intermediate probe agreement, unrelated to the
social-psychology term \citep{ross1977false}.} This
reconciles the aggregate savings above: those savings are real, but they ride on
a \emph{directional} accuracy cost that safety-blind, probe-cost-free evaluation
averages away.

The gap has a concrete source. A model that has not finished reasoning is
nonetheless \emph{forced} by the probe to write an answer, and the cheapest thing
to write is what it wrote last time; this elicited placeholder can repeat across
many probes and look settled while the underlying trajectory goes on to correct
it. Three direct measurements bear this out (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}):
re-probing the \emph{same} frozen prefix with a differently worded query returns a
different answer $57\%$ of the time in the first tenth of a trajectory against
$11\%$ near the end, so early ``answers'' are largely artifacts of being asked;
hand-labelling $134$ wrong stops finds three in five commit to an answer the model
had not converged on, against one in five it had genuinely settled; and continued
reasoning recovers a correct answer far more often than a stop banks a wrong
one---up to ${\sim}\mathbf{45}\times$ at an aggressive latest-probe stop. Widening
the agreement window does not close the gap: it lowers the loss only by firing
later and less often, so at the largest windows the ratio falls to
${\sim}2\times$ while the rule saves almost nothing.

To rule out that this is a badly chosen threshold or a single implementation, we
search the signal exhaustively under commitment. On frozen trajectories from two
models and three benchmarks we run a \emph{preregistered} sweep of the windowed
consensus family we study---window size $\times$ share threshold plus operational
knobs, $3{,}520$ rules---with problem-grouped splits and acceptance gates fixed in
advance (Section~\ref{sec:results}). No setting of these knobs
closes the gap: \textbf{not one of the $3{,}520$ rules is both safe and
saving---none clears any gate}. The frontier reproduces on a held-out test split
($r{=}0.98$) and on unseen models at $4\times$ the scale and a different
architecture, and a shipped operating point (CertaIndex (CoT)) loses
$56$--$70\pp$ on the same trajectories.

That the gap cannot be tuned away leaves one alternative explanation---that early
exit is simply impossible at these budgets. It is not. As a positive control we
sweep one non-consensus method---DEER, which reads the model's confidence at a
reasoning boundary---through the identical pipeline and gates: it clears all
three, losing as little as $0.3\pp$ while saving $28\%$, and does so on the unseen
models too. DEER is a control, not a proposal; it establishes that early exit is
achievable, so the consensus family's failure is specific to \emph{what agreement
measures}. Figure~\ref{fig:idea} is the argument in one picture. Our claim
concerns agreement used \emph{alone} as a stopping criterion
(\S\ref{sec:results}).

\paragraph{Contribution.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{We identify a stability--terminality gap in probe-based early
  exit}: repeated intermediate agreement establishes that the current answer
  \emph{persists} under a fixed probing procedure, not that the reasoning has
  \emph{terminated} (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}).
  \item \textbf{We explain what agreement actually measures}, through three direct
  signals---the wording sensitivity of the extracted answer, a hand-labelled error
  taxonomy of stopped-but-wrong cases ($\kappa{=}0.82$), and a trajectory-level
  recovery asymmetry---which together show that early agreement is a
  probe-elicited placeholder on an unfinished trajectory rather than a settled
  belief (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}).
  \item \textbf{We show the gap is not a threshold or implementation artifact}: a
  preregistered sweep of $3{,}520$ rules on frozen trajectories closes no gate,
  the frontier reproduces on a held-out split and two unseen models, and a
  non-consensus positive control (DEER) swept under the identical protocol clears
  the same gates---ruling out that early exit is simply impossible
  (Section~\ref{sec:results}). We release the protocol,
  trajectories, and sweep in full.
\end{itemize}
