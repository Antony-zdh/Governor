\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Reasoning language models can spend thousands of tokens on a single answer.
Early exit---halting a chain of thought once its answer is settled---could reduce
that cost, but only if a stopping signal is safe. A common signal is
\emph{intermediate answer consensus}: at regular intervals, a partial trajectory
is probed for its current answer and generation is stopped when recent probes
agree \citep{dynasor_certaindex}. The underlying assumption is simple: if the
model repeatedly says $x$, it has probably finished reasoning about $x$. This
paper asks whether that assumption is warranted: \textbf{is intermediate
agreement a safe signal on which to stop?}\footnote{We use ``false consensus'' for the phenomenon that
intermediate probe agreement is non-terminal; the term is unrelated to the
social-psychology ``false consensus effect'' \citep{ross1977false}. We study
answer-level early exit (stopping a chain of thought), not token- or layer-level
early exit \citep{graves2016act,schuster2022confident}.}

We find that it is not. The issue is not a poorly chosen threshold: intermediate
consensus often appears while the trajectory is still changing. On frozen
reasoning trajectories, stopping at consensus destroys a correct final answer
about $35$ times more often than it preserves an otherwise wrong one. To test
whether careful rule design can avoid this failure, we preregister a
seven-dimensional family of consensus rules, evaluation splits, and acceptance
gates, then evaluate all $17{,}712$ rules on two models and three benchmarks.
Every rule has a positive worst-case accuracy loss; none reaches break-even. The
same frontier appears on a held-out split and on two unseen models. Thus the
paper's claim is deliberately narrow but strong: within this broad, precommitted
family, agreement reports what the model has said recently, not whether its
reasoning has finished.

The rest of the paper is organized around this question. Section~\ref{sec:related}
places consensus stopping among related early-exit methods. Section~\ref{sec:method}
defines the frozen-trajectory evaluation and preregistered rule family.
Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the empirical result, including held-out
evaluations. Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} then examines the counterfactual cost of
stopping and separates it from probe overhead; Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}
states the resulting scope and implication.
