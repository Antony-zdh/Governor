\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:results}

We first confirm the phenomenon the paper turns on---intermediate consensus is
non-terminal (\S\ref{sec:exp-fc})---then report the preregistered sweep and its
central negative result (\S\ref{sec:exp-sweep}), reproduce three prior
early-stoppers on identical trajectories (\S\ref{sec:exp-prior}), and confirm the
frontier out of distribution (\S\ref{sec:exp-heldout}).

\subsection{False consensus is real}
\label{sec:exp-fc}
We log a dense probe stream for every \textsc{MATH}500 problem under \dsseven{},
never intervening---the probe is a separate forward pass that only observes the
main trajectory (full setup in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-fc})---and read off four
facts.

\textbf{(1) Agreement is not correctness.} \emph{Cumulative} agreement (all
non-empty probes so far concur) is nearly trustworthy ($98.9\%$ correct on the
$87$ problems reaching it), but that is not what an online rule sees. The
operative signal, \emph{window} agreement (the last five probes concurring), is
markedly less reliable: of the $338$ problems reaching a unanimous last-five
window, only $93.5\%$ are correct---a $6.5\%$ false-consensus rate.
\textbf{(2) Recovery is common.} Of the $375$ problems whose \emph{first} probe is
wrong, $76.3\%$ eventually flip to correct; of the $145$ that formed a three-probe
consensus \emph{different} from their final answer, $65.5\%$ ended up correct.
\textbf{(3) Later consensus is worse.} Consensus formed before $512$ tokens is
$87.4\%$ correct, but consensus forming only after $2048$ tokens is $58.1\%$
correct---hard problems converge slowly \emph{and} to worse answers.
\textbf{(4) The cost lands at the stop.} A naive stop (first three consecutive
agreeing, certain, non-empty probes) fires on $416/500$ problems and commits to
answers correct only $69.2\%$ of the time, whereas letting those same problems run
to completion reaches $85.6\%$: a $16.4\pp$ loss.\footnote{This illustration uses a
tight $3072$-token budget so truncation is visible on a single A100 and a
simplified heuristic, \emph{not} the Certaindex algorithm (\S\ref{sec:exp-prior}).
The main study below runs at the $16$K/$32$K budgets used throughout, where the
same effect appears as the directional accuracy tax of
Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}.}

A good stopping rule must therefore be much more than ``agreement.'' The rest of
this section asks whether the \emph{best possible} such rule, drawn from a large
preregistered space, can be both safe and economical.

\subsection{The safe-and-saving corner is empty}
\label{sec:exp-sweep}
We aggregate the sweep into per-environment metrics---$637{,}632$ rows
$=17{,}712$ rules $\times$ $18$ environments $\times$ $2$ splits (train, dev)---
and compute, per rule, its worst per-model and per-benchmark accuracy drop and its
net token savings (accounting of Section~\ref{sec:accounting}). Applying
commitment~C2 to the preregistered conservative gate yields the paper's central
result, and it does not rest on any single fragile estimate.

The headline is distribution-free: \emph{every} one of the $17{,}712$ candidate
rules incurs a positive worst-case per-model accuracy drop, and \emph{zero} reach
break-even. Across all $637{,}632$ rule--environment evaluations, accuracy
strictly \emph{falls} in $67.7\%$ and strictly \emph{rises} in only $6.7\%$
(unchanged in $25.5\%$), for a mean drop of $10.9\pp$; over rules the worst-case
per-model drop has percentiles $p_1{=}3.37$, $p_5{=}4.26$, $p_{25}{=}10.7$, median
$20.1\pp$. The Pareto frontier over (worst-case drop, net savings) contains $93$
rules and \textbf{none clears the conservative gate} (per-model drop $\le1.5\pp$,
per-benchmark $\le2.0\pp$, psf $\ge0.8$). Figure~\ref{fig:pareto} shows the shape:
the boundary only climbs as the accuracy drop grows, and the small-drop /
large-saving corner is empty. Two claims should be kept separate: the safety claim
(no rule reaches even $1.5\pp$; all lose) is about accuracy and, as
Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} argues, is probe-independent; the savings claim
(positive net savings costs $\ge4.87\pp$, whether the psf floor is $0.8/0.7/0.6/0.5$)
additionally depends on the dense-probe accounting.

\paragraph{On the least-bad rule (and why we do not lean on it).}
\label{sec:floor}
The smallest per-model drop anywhere is $1.85\pp$---already above the gate---from
an \texttt{entropy\_budget\_fraction} rule that stops so rarely its net savings are
negative ($q_{20}\approx-11\%$). We report it as a diagnostic, not a load-bearing
number: a bootstrap over the nine benchmark$\times$seed environments puts it at
$1.85\pp$ with a $95\%$ CI of $[0.0,5.6]\pp$, touching break-even. We rest the
result instead on the two facts that do not depend on this point estimate---all
$17{,}712$ rules lose accuracy, and even this least-bad rule spends \emph{extra}
tokens (net $-8$ to $-9\%$)---and on the held-out reproduction of
\S\ref{sec:exp-heldout}. The per-model gate is primary; the per-benchmark cap is a
diagnostic a single competition-set problem cannot resolve
(Section~\ref{sec:resolution}).

\paragraph{Sophistication does not help.}
The ordering across families is one-directional: the simplest evidence signal
(\texttt{window\_share}) gives the best safe-with-positive-savings point
($4.87\pp$); \texttt{entropy\_budget\_fraction} reaches $1.85\pp$ only by stopping
so rarely savings go negative; and \texttt{adaptive\_event\_probe}---the most
sophisticated family---is \emph{dominated}, needing $9.70\pp$ for a
positive-savings point (frontiers in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-tables}).
Table~\ref{tab:main} places our best swept points against full generation; none
passes the gate, and even the accuracy-neutral point spends $\sim\!4\%$ extra
tokens (probe tax, Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}).

\subsection{Prior stoppers on the same trajectories}
\label{sec:exp-prior}
To place published methods on the same footing, we reproduce three in a common
harness---the same frozen trajectories, the same \simplek{} probe bank, the same
token accounting. \textbf{CertaIndex} \citep{dynasor_certaindex} is the faithful
share-threshold stop (the direct descendant of the swept family); \textbf{DEER}
\citep{deer} stops on the model's own confidence in a trial answer at reasoning
boundaries---a \emph{different} signal; \textbf{TJE} \citep{tje} is a token-level
stop. Table~\ref{tab:baselines} reports the reproductions (not the original
papers' end-to-end runs).

\begin{table*}[t]
  \centering\small
  \begin{tabular}{llccccc}

    \midrule
    Full generation & \qweneight{}   & $85.4\%$ & --- & $0\%$ & $0\%$ & --- \\
    Full generation & \dsseven{} & $79.8\%$ & --- & $0\%$ & $0\%$ & --- \\
    \midrule
    CertaIndex & \qweneight{}   & $15.3\%$ & $-70.1\pp$ & $90.1\%$ & $99.8\%$ & consensus \\
    CertaIndex & \dsseven{} & $23.9\%$ & $-55.9\pp$ & $76.7\%$ & $98.7\%$ & consensus \\
    TJE        & \qweneight{}   & $85.0\%$ & $-0.4\pp$  & $2.0\%$  & $22.5\%$ & token-level \\
    TJE        & \dsseven{} & $60.7\%$ & $-19.1\pp$ & $65.0\%$ & $93.4\%$ & token-level \\

  \end{tabular}

  over three benchmarks; probe tokens charged). CertaIndex---the consensus
  descendant---\textbf{collapses} ($-56$ to $-70\pp$) while saving $77$--$90\%$ of
  tokens: it fires on almost every problem, very early, exactly the
  false-consensus trap of \S\ref{sec:exp-fc}. \textbf{DEER}, which reads a

CertaIndex, at $99.8\%$ stop rate and $70\pp$ lost, is the accuracy tax paid in
full; in Figure~\ref{fig:pareto} it sits at the far right of the collapse, while
DEER---outside the swept space---occupies the low-drop corner. TJE is unsafe on
the weaker model. That only the non-consensus method survives is the crux we
analyze in Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}.

\subsection{The frontier reproduces out of distribution}
\label{sec:exp-heldout}
We read the test split once (commitment~C3). Because the development result is that
\emph{no} rule clears the gate, there is nothing to confirm by re-selection; we ask
whether the \emph{frontier} reproduces, ranking rules on dev and only measuring on
test.

\paragraph{Held-out test split.}
Across all $17{,}712$ rules, each rule's worst-case per-model drop on test tracks
its dev value ($r=0.96$), and every rule still loses. Toward the decision boundary
the correlation is noise-limited on both sides ($r\approx0$ for dev drop
$\le5\pp$; the $144$ rules with dev drop $\le3\pp$ collapse to a single test value,
$+0.11\pp$). The split-invariant claim is the empty joint gate: \textbf{no rule
clears the conservative gate on both splits}---$0$ on dev, and of the $272$ rules
admissible on \emph{test alone}, not one is admissible on dev (there they cost
$4.98$--$5.65\pp$, median $5.09$). These $272$ are exactly the in-sample winners
the held-out split exists to reject.

\paragraph{Unseen scale ($4\times$) and architecture.}
On \mbox{Distill-Qwen-32B}, never used in development, the frontier reproduces
($r=0.903$) and the dev-selected ``safe'' rules do not transfer: rules with dev
drop $\le1.5\pp$ cost a mean $+2.58\pp$ and up to $+8.33\pp$ on test, and the dev
floor rule drops $+8.33\pp$ there. Scaling $4\times$ opens no safe corner. The
held-out architecture (\mbox{DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B}) reproduces the same
qualitative pattern.\footnote{The Llama-8B trajectories were re-collected after
correcting a chat-template defect (a missing model-specific beginning-of-sequence
token that degraded its generations); the corrected model scores $\sim\!88\%$ on
\textsc{MATH}500. Full corrected numbers are in the released sweep.} Across the
held-out split and both unseen models, no rule is jointly safe-and-saving.
