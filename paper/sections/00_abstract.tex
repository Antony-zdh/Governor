\begin{abstract}
A natural way to cut reasoning-model inference cost is to probe the partial
trajectory for its current answer and stop once probes \emph{agree}. We show
that this signal measures the wrong object: agreement establishes that the
current answer \emph{persists} under a fixed probing procedure, not that the
reasoning has \emph{terminated}---a \emph{stability--terminality gap}. A model
that has not finished reasoning is still forced by the probe to answer, and
this elicited placeholder can repeat and look settled while the trajectory goes
on to correct it: re-wording the probe changes the early answer $57\%$ of the
time, three in five hand-labelled wrong stops commit to an answer the model had
not converged on, and stopping destroys a correct-in-the-end answer up to
${\sim}\mathbf{45\times}$ more often than it banks a wrong one---widening the
agreement window lowers the loss only by firing later and less often. A
\emph{preregistered} sweep of $3{,}520$ consensus rules on frozen trajectories
clears none of three acceptance gates fixed in advance, and the failure
reproduces on a held-out split and two unseen models. A boundary-confidence control
(DEER), swept through the identical pipeline, clears all three---the failure is
the signal's, not early exit's. Agreement fails not because it is
insufficiently strict, but because it repeatedly measures the wrong object.
\end{abstract}
