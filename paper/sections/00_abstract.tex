\begin{abstract}
A natural and increasingly studied way to cut reasoning-model inference cost is to
probe the partial trajectory for its current answer and stop once probes
\emph{agree}. We show that repeated agreement is not a safe stopping criterion on
its own, because it measures the wrong object: under a fixed probing procedure,
agreement establishes that the current answer \emph{persists}, not that the
reasoning has \emph{terminated}---a \emph{stability--terminality gap}. The gap has
a concrete source. A model that has not finished reasoning is still \emph{forced}
by the probe to emit an answer, and the cheapest thing to emit is what it emitted
last time; this elicited placeholder can repeat for many probes and look settled,
yet the trajectory goes on to correct it. We show this directly: re-probing the
\emph{same} frozen prefix with a differently worded query changes the extracted
answer $57\%$ of the time early in a trajectory (against $11\%$ near the end),
hand-labelling $134$ wrong stops finds three in five commit to an answer the model
had not converged on, and continued reasoning recovers a correct answer far more
often than a stop banks a wrong one (up to ${\sim}\mathbf{45\times}$ at an
aggressive latest-probe stop, ${\sim}2\times$ at the largest windows, where the
rule has nearly stopped firing). Widening the agreement window therefore does not
make the signal better at detecting terminality; it only lowers the loss by firing
later and less often. A \emph{preregistered} sweep of $3{,}520$ consensus rules
(window size, share threshold, and operational knobs; frozen trajectories from two
models and three math benchmarks; acceptance gates fixed in advance) shows no
setting of these knobs closes the gap: \textbf{no rule clears any gate}. As a
positive control that early exit is not itself impossible, a boundary-confidence
method (DEER) swept through the identical pipeline \textbf{clears all three gates}
and holds on a held-out split and two unseen models. Agreement fails not because it
is insufficiently strict, but because it repeatedly measures the wrong object. We
release the protocol, the frozen trajectories, and the full sweep.
\end{abstract}
