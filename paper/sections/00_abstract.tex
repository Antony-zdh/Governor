\begin{abstract}
Reasoning language models spend most of their tokens \emph{after} the answer is
effectively decided, so a popular way to cut inference cost is to periodically
probe the partial trajectory for its current answer and stop once probes
\emph{agree}. We show this bet is unsafe, and that the failure is a property of
the \emph{signal}, not of the stopping threshold. On frozen reasoning
trajectories from two models and three math benchmarks we run a
\emph{preregistered} search over a seven-dimensional consensus-rule schema
($17{,}712$ rules) with problem-grouped splits and acceptance gates fixed in
advance. The result is distribution-free: \textbf{every one of the $17{,}712$
rules loses accuracy in the worst case}---none reaches break-even, and stopping
strictly lowers accuracy in $67.7\%$ of rule--environment evaluations. The reason
is directional, not statistical: intermediate consensus forms \emph{while the
answer is still moving}, so a stop destroys a correct-in-the-end answer roughly
$\mathbf{35}$~\textbf{times more often} than it rescues a wrong one---an
\emph{accuracy tax} that is a property of the trajectory, not the probe, and that
no threshold in the searched space escapes. The same frontier reproduces on a
held-out test split ($r{=}0.96$) and on unseen models at $4\times$ the scale and
a different architecture, with no rule safe on both splits; a faithful Certaindex
reproduction on identical trajectories collapses by $56$--$70\pp$. The failure is
specific to \emph{consensus}: a method reading a different signal---the model's
own confidence at reasoning-boundary tokens---stays near full accuracy on the
same trajectories, which localizes the problem to probe agreement and indicates
where a safe early-exit signal must instead come from. We release the protocol,
the frozen trajectories, and the full sweep.
\end{abstract}
