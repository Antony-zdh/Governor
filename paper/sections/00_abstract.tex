\begin{abstract}
A popular way to cut reasoning-model inference cost is to probe the partial
trajectory for its current answer and stop once probes \emph{agree}. We show this
is unsafe as a stopping criterion on its own, and explain why. Self-consistency
earns its guarantee from \emph{independently sampled} reasoning paths; probing one
trajectory reads the same prefix repeatedly, so agreement carries no vote that can
outweigh an error. Hand-labelling $134$ wrong stops shows what agreement actually
records: three in five commit to an answer the model had not converged on---a
placeholder the probe compelled it to emit---and only one in five to an answer it
had genuinely settled on. The cost is directional: a stop destroys a
correct-in-the-end answer far more often than it rescues a wrong one (up to
${\sim}\mathbf{45\times}$ at an aggressive latest-probe stop, ${\sim}2\times$ at
the largest windows, where the rule has almost stopped firing). No tuning escapes
this: on frozen trajectories from two models and three math benchmarks, a
\emph{preregistered} sweep of the consensus signal ($3{,}520$ rules, acceptance
gates fixed in advance) clears \textbf{no gate}. The failure is specific to the
signal---swept identically, a boundary-confidence method (DEER) \textbf{clears
all three gates} and holds on a held-out split and two unseen models. Early exit
is possible; agreement alone cannot make it safe. We release the protocol, the
frozen trajectories, and the full sweep.
\end{abstract}
