\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We asked whether intermediate answer-consensus is a safe signal on which to
early-exit a reasoning LLM. Used on its own it is not, and not because the rule
is untuned: under a fixed probing procedure agreement establishes that the
current answer \emph{persists}, not that the reasoning has \emph{terminated}---a
\emph{stability--terminality gap}. What the probes agree on is often an answer
the model has not settled on---a placeholder the probe forced it to
produce---and stopping on it destroys a correct-in-the-end answer far more often
than it rescues a wrong one (up to ${\sim}45\times$ at an aggressive
latest-probe stop, ${\sim}2\times$ at the largest windows, where the rule has
nearly stopped firing), so demanding more agreement lowers the loss only by
declining to fire. The asymmetry is a property of the trajectory, and it shows
up as an empty safe-and-saving corner across a preregistered space of
$3{,}520$ rules---reproduced on a held-out split and two unseen models, and
visible at one shipped operating point in CertaIndex (CoT)---while a
boundary-confidence control swept through the identical protocol clears every
gate: the failure is the signal's, not early exit's.
Repeated agreement under a fixed probing procedure establishes answer
persistence, not reasoning terminality; within the broad preregistered family
studied here, making that agreement stricter only trades away stopping
opportunities rather than turning it into a safe stopping signal. Agreement
fails not because it is insufficiently strict, but because it repeatedly
measures the wrong object.
