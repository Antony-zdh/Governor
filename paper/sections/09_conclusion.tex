\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We asked whether intermediate answer-consensus is a safe signal on which to
early-exit a reasoning LLM. Used on its own it is not, and not because the rule is
untuned. Agreement is informative in self-consistency because the paths are
sampled independently; probes of one trajectory share a prefix, so they cannot
outvote each other's errors. What they agree on is often an answer the model has
not settled on: three in five of our hand-labelled wrong stops commit to a
placeholder the probe forced it to produce. Stopping on such an answer destroys a
correct-in-the-end answer far more often than it rescues a wrong one
(up to ${\sim}45\times$ at an aggressive latest-probe stop, ${\sim}2\times$ at the
largest windows, where the rule has nearly stopped firing). This accuracy tax is a property of the trajectory rather than the
probe, and it shows up as an empty safe-and-saving corner across a preregistered
space of $3{,}520$ rules---not one of which clears any gate---reproduced on a
held-out split and two unseen models, and visible at one shipped operating point
in CertaIndex (CoT), which loses $56$--$70\pp$. That the corner is empty for
consensus but not for early exit is the point: swept through the identical
protocol, a method reading the model's own boundary confidence (DEER) clears all
three gates on the very same trajectories, so the failure is specific to
consensus. The takeaway for anyone building safe early exit is therefore about
the choice of signal: read whether the trajectory itself has settled, not whether
its recent probes agree. This does not put probe agreement beyond use---it is
cheap, and it does report that the current answer has not changed. What our
results rule out is treating that report, by itself, as evidence that the
reasoning is done.
