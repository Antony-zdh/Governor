\section{Analysis}
\label{sec:mechanism}

Section~\ref{sec:results} shows that no rule in the searched space is both safe
and saving. This section gives the reason, which is what makes the result more
than a report on $3{,}520$ particular rules: a \emph{stability--terminality gap}.
Under a fixed probing procedure, agreement establishes that the current answer
\emph{persists}, not that the reasoning has \emph{terminated}. The source is that
a probe does not read a settled belief---when the reasoning is unfinished it
\emph{forces} an answer, and the model's cheapest output is a repeat of its last
one, an elicited placeholder that can look stable while the trajectory keeps
correcting itself. We establish this directly, and in this order: the answer a
probe returns is sensitive to how the probe is worded early on
(\S\ref{sec:mech-wording}); a hand-labelled taxonomy shows most stopped-but-wrong
commitments are to answers the model had not converged on
(\S\ref{sec:mech-placeholder}); and the correction that follows makes the cost
\emph{directional}, so widening the window---the obvious remedy---reduces the loss
only by declining to stop (\S\ref{sec:mech-ratio}). A structural precondition
underlies all three: probe readings are not independent samples, so agreement
supplies no vote that could catch the error (\S\ref{sec:mech-independence}). The
resulting recovery asymmetry is a property of the reasoning trajectory, not of a
particular threshold.

\subsection{Probe Wording versus Position}
\label{sec:mech-wording}
If early agreement reflected a settled belief, the answer a probe returns should
not depend much on how the probe is worded. We test this directly on paired
re-probes of the \emph{same frozen prefixes}: at every probe position we read the
trajectory with two different probe wordings (our boxed-answer suffix and the
CertaIndex suffix, both capped at $32$ tokens), so the model's state is identical
and only the elicitation differs.

Figure~\ref{fig:wording-taxonomy}(a) shows the result. In the first tenth of a trajectory the
two wordings return different answers $57\%$ of the time; by the final third they
disagree only $11\%$ of the time. Early ``answers'' are therefore substantially a
property of the question asked rather than of the state being read, and they
become properties of the state only as the trajectory finishes.

This distinguishes our account from the obvious alternative. If the model simply
held a stable but mistaken belief early on, both wordings would read back the
\emph{same} wrong value; instead they disagree. What a consensus rule treats as a
converging answer is, early on, an output the probe has elicited. Note also that
probe wording has almost no effect on \emph{final} accuracy in the same paired
data (a $0.65\pp$ readout effect against a $9.15\pp$ effect of \emph{when} one
probes), so the sensitivity above is specific to early positions and is not a
defect of any particular suffix.

\subsection{Error Taxonomy}
\label{sec:mech-placeholder}
Two annotators independently labelled all $134$ stopped-but-wrong cases
(Figure~\ref{fig:wording-taxonomy}(b), $\kappa = 0.82$): only one in five is a case where
the model had genuinely settled on a wrong value. Three in five are answers it
had not converged on at all, and a further one in six are artifacts of the
probe's output format.

The reason is that a probe does not read the model's belief; it \emph{forces} an
answer. A model that has not finished reasoning must still write something in the
box, and the cheapest thing to write is what it wrote last time. Repetition of a
forced answer is therefore stable for reasons that have nothing to do with the
reasoning being finished, and a consensus rule cannot tell that case apart from a
settled one. This also predicts the window effect of \S\ref{sec:mech-ratio}: a
longer window is a longer buffer in which a placeholder can be overturned, which
is why it reduces false consensus without removing the underlying risk.

\subsection{Effect of Window Size}
\label{sec:mech-ratio}
The natural remedy is to demand more agreement before stopping. It helps, but
only by stopping less often. Consider only the stops where committing early \emph{changes} correctness relative
to the trajectory's own final answer, and count two outcomes: (final-correct,
stop-wrong)---a correct recovery destroyed---versus (final-wrong,
stop-correct)---a wrong answer luckily banked. Pure sampling noise predicts a
$\approx1{:}1$ ratio; a genuine ``continued reasoning corrects'' effect predicts
$\gg1$. On the frozen replay this ratio depends strongly on the window: it falls
from $\approx\!45{:}1$ for a latest-probe stop ($W{=}1$) to $\approx\!2{:}1$ at
the largest window ($W{=}30$). But the low end is bought only by stopping so
rarely that net saving vanishes ($W{=}30$ fires on $121$ dev problems vs.\ $668$
at $W{=}1$); at any window that actually saves tokens the ratio stays well above
$1{:}1$. DEER, by contrast, holds $\approx\!2.4$--$3.5{:}1$ at its three operating
points \emph{while} saving $28$--$32\%$, because its boundary-confidence signal
commits mainly once the trajectory has settled. Stopping on consensus therefore
removes a correct-in-the-end answer many times more often than it rescues a wrong
one---directional, not sampling noise. This is the recovery phenomenon of
\S\ref{sec:exp-fc} ($84.2\%$, $89.1\%$), now at the main $16$K/$32$K budgets and
as a signed effect. Figure~\ref{fig:harm-rescue} plots the whole window axis, so
the claim rests on the shape of the curve rather than on either endpoint: the
ratio and the saving fall together, and DEER sits at consensus's best ratio and
its best saving simultaneously, which no window does.

\subsection{Case Studies}
\label{sec:mech-cases}
The three regimes above appear together on individual trajectories
(\dsseven{}, \textsc{MATH}500, probes every $64$ tokens; $a\times n$ denotes an
answer repeated over $n$ consecutive probes). Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-cases}
gives the full streams and discussion.

On problem~$320$ the model answers \texttt{0} at its \emph{first} probe and holds
it for $27$ probes---nearly half a $3{,}683$-token trajectory---before reasoning
its way to the correct $-\tfrac{24}{25}$. The committed value is not a considered
wrong answer but one emitted before any real work, and its stability is
correspondingly uninformative. The run is also long enough to defeat every window
we searched: even $W{=}24$ fires here and commits \texttt{0}, which is why
widening the window is not a remedy for a placeholder that outlasts it.

Problem~$253$ asks what fraction of two feet is three inches, a free-response
question with no options. Every probe from token $384$ to token $1600$ returns the
letter \texttt{D}; the correct $\tfrac{1}{8}$ appears only at the final probe.
Agreement is total and refers to nothing, which is the format artifact of
\S\ref{sec:mech-placeholder} in its clearest form.

The minority case is worth stating too. On problem~$240$ a three-probe rule
commits \texttt{52} while the trajectory itself ends at \texttt{154}; the gold
answer is $116$, so both are wrong and stopping destroys nothing. This is the
regime consensus early exit was designed for---the model was not going to solve
the problem, so a stop is free. The difficulty is that agreement cannot tell this
case apart from problem~$320$, and problem~$320$ is the more common one.

\subsection{Why the recovery asymmetry is trajectory-level}
Why does the ratio stay above one across the grid? The recovery it counts is a
property of the reasoning trajectory: for a stop at token position $s$, the
correction forgone is whatever the trajectory would have made after $s$, which
depends on the trajectory, not on how it was probed. The \emph{realized} accuracy
cost of a rule is not itself probe-independent---it depends on where the rule
stops, and hence on the probe schedule and stopping rule---but the recovery
asymmetry that produces it is present at every position where reasoning is still
moving. Our probe grid (intervals $64/128/256/512$ tokens) makes essentially every
stop position reachable, so the floor we observe is the minimum over these
schedules rather than a lucky miss; a sparser schedule can only stop later or at a
subset of positions, never at a safer one the grid missed. Hence no threshold on
the consensus signal \emph{within the searched space} escapes the tax. This does
not turn a finite rule search into a universal impossibility theorem---it motivates
caution for consensus variants beyond the preregistered schema rather than proving
a universal claim about them. In short, agreement reports what the model has said
recently, not whether its reasoning has finished.

\subsection{Accuracy Tax versus Probe Tax}
The natural rebuttal is that the negative result is an artifact of charging dense
re-probing. It is not, and separating two costs shows why. Besides the accuracy
tax, a rule pays a \emph{probe tax}: the probe output $p$ (Section~\ref{sec:accounting}).
Under dense re-probing a late-stopping large-window rule pays $p$ over a long
prefix, opening a gap between its \emph{gross} savings and its (smaller)
\emph{net} savings (Table~\ref{tab:grossnet}). Unlike the accuracy tax, the probe
tax scales with probe density and is reducible (sparser or shorter probes,
KV-cache reuse). The two are separable: the \emph{savings} axis is partly a
probe-tax artifact and is addressable, but the \emph{accuracy} floor is intrinsic
to the trajectory---present wherever reasoning is still moving---and is what makes
the corner empty; it is that floor, not the probe tax, that DEER escapes.

\subsection{The role of independence}
\label{sec:mech-independence}
A structural fact underlies why a bad stop is never caught. Self-consistency's
majority vote can absorb an error because its paths are sampled independently
(\S\ref{sec:related}): a wrong answer on one path is outvoted by others that
reasoned differently. Probing one trajectory yields no such thing---every probe
reads the same prefix, so the readings move together, and when the prefix is wrong
they are wrong together. Agreement across probes is thus a statement about the
stability of one line of reasoning, not corroboration from several, so a consensus
rule holds no vote in reserve to overturn the placeholder it commits to.
Independence explains why the gap is \emph{dangerous} rather than merely present;
it does not on its own show the committed answer is non-terminal---that is what the
direct measurements above establish.

\subsection{Locating the Failure}
The two experiments of \S\ref{sec:exp-deer}--\ref{sec:exp-prior} localize the
cause. Every method that stops on \emph{probe agreement}---the swept consensus
family and the CertaIndex (CoT) reproduction alike---fails a gate or loses many points;
the one method that clears the gates, DEER, reads not probe agreement but the
model's own confidence in a trial answer at a reasoning boundary. Because DEER is
swept through the \emph{identical} pipeline, gates, and token accounting, this is
a controlled test: the same early-exit machinery fails on the consensus signal and
succeeds on a non-consensus one, so the failure is a property of the
\emph{signal}, not of early exit.

What this does \emph{not} establish is which signal should replace agreement. We
show that agreement alone is insufficient; we do not show that any particular
alternative is sufficient, and DEER is a control rather than a recommendation.
Identifying and validating a safe stopping signal---with its own
train/dev$\rightarrow$test confirmation---is left open.

That qualification matters. Our claim is about consensus used as the sole
criterion for stopping, which is the use we searched and the use that fails.
Probe agreement is cheap to compute and does carry one piece of information---that
the current answer has not changed---and combined with a signal that reflects the
model's own estimate it may well be useful; CertaIndex composes multiple
indicators for related reasons \citep{dynasor_certaindex}. Nothing here argues
against such combinations. What the sweep rules out is the step of treating
agreement, on its own, as sufficient evidence that reasoning has finished.

Two further caveats bound the claim: it concerns \emph{consensus-based} rules
within the searched schema and dense-probe regime, and it does not say early exit
is impossible---only that this signal cannot make it safe alone.
