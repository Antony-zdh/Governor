\section{Mechanism: Two Taxes, One Intrinsic}
\label{sec:mechanism}

Why is the safe-and-saving corner empty? We separate two distinct costs of
stopping and show that only one of them depends on how we probe.

\subsection{Accounting}
For a rule that stops a problem at token position $s$ after consuming probe
output $p$, we charge total decode tokens $T = s + p$ and compare against the
frozen baseline $B$ (the full trajectory, capped at budget). Net savings is
$(B-T)/B$; the accuracy drop compares the committed answer at $s$ against the
frozen final answer. Two quantities therefore move independently: \emph{where}
the rule stops (which sets both the accuracy drop and the main-token saving
$s$) and \emph{how much it probes} to get there (which adds $p$).

\subsection{The accuracy tax is intrinsic}
The accuracy drop depends only on the stop position $s$, not on $p$: committing
to the answer at $s$ forgoes whatever correction the trajectory would have made
after $s$. This is the quantity that never reaches zero---the $1.85\pp$ floor of
Section~\ref{sec:results} is an \emph{accuracy tax} that no probing scheme can
remove, because it is a property of the reasoning trajectory, not of the probe.
A \emph{direction-of-effect} test makes this concrete: among stops where the
committed answer differs from the final answer, we measure the ratio of
(final-correct, stop-wrong) to (final-wrong, stop-correct). Pure sampling noise
would give $\approx 1{:}1$; a genuine ``continued reasoning corrects'' effect
gives a ratio $\gg 1$. We observe \pending{direction-of-effect ratio, offline
compute} , consistent with the recovery statistics of
Section~\ref{sec:falseconsensus} and inconsistent with the drop being an
inference-noise artifact.

\subsection{The probe tax is a design choice}
The second cost is the probe output $p$. Under dense re-probing (every $64$
tokens, $32$-token probes), a rule that stops late pays $p$ over a long prefix,
which is what turns the \emph{net} savings of the safe (entropy) family negative
even though its \emph{gross} savings $(B-s)/B$ is positive. Unlike the accuracy
tax, the probe tax scales with probe density and is reducible (sparser probes,
shorter probes, or KV-cache reuse). We therefore report savings two ways:

\subsection{Why the distinction matters}
Separating the taxes pre-empts the natural objection that our negative result is
an artifact of dense probing. It is not: the \emph{savings collapse} is partly a
probe-tax artifact and is addressable, but the \emph{accuracy floor}---the
finding that even the safest stop costs $\ge 1.85\pp$---is probe-independent and
survives any probing scheme. The two families of prior work we test next fail on
the accuracy axis, not the probe axis.
