\section{The Stability--Terminality Gap}
\label{sec:mechanism}

This section establishes the paper's central object by direct measurement,
before any rule sweep: under a fixed probing procedure, agreement establishes
that the current answer \emph{persists}, not that the reasoning has
\emph{terminated}. The source is that a probe does not read a settled
belief---when the reasoning is unfinished it \emph{forces} an answer, and the
model's cheapest output is a repeat of its last one, an elicited placeholder
that can look stable while the trajectory keeps correcting itself. We establish, in order: intermediate agreement is pervasive
and non-terminal (\S\ref{sec:exp-fc}); the early answer depends on how the
probe is worded (\S\ref{sec:mech-wording}); most stopped-but-wrong commitments
are to answers the model had not converged on (\S\ref{sec:mech-placeholder});
and the ensuing correction makes the cost \emph{directional}, so widening the
window reduces the loss only by declining to stop (\S\ref{sec:mech-ratio}),
while non-independent probe readings supply no vote that could catch the error
(\S\ref{sec:mech-independence}).

\subsection{Agreement Is Not Terminal}
\label{sec:exp-fc}
We log a dense probe stream for every \textsc{MATH}500 problem under \dsseven{}
at the main $16$K/$32$K budgets (all $500$ problems, three seeds; $1{,}500$
trajectories), never intervening (full setup in
Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-fc}), and read off four facts.
\textbf{(1) Final agreement is reliable; early agreement is not.} By the time a
trajectory finishes, agreement does track correctness: cumulative agreement
across all probes is $97.8\%$ correct, and even the final five-probe window is
$90.4\%$ correct. But an online rule cannot wait for the end---it fires the
\emph{first} time probes agree, mid-trajectory, where the answer is far from
settled.
\textbf{(2) Recovery is common.} Of the $1{,}148$ trajectories whose first
probe is wrong, $89.1\%$ eventually flip to correct; of the $736$ that formed a
three-probe consensus \emph{different} from their final answer, $84.2\%$ ended
up correct.
\textbf{(3) Later consensus is worse.} Consensus formed before $512$ tokens is
$91.0\%$ correct, but consensus forming only after $2048$ tokens is $71.6\%$
correct---hard problems converge slowly \emph{and} to worse answers.
\textbf{(4) The cost lands at the stop.} A naive stop (first three consecutive
agreeing, certain, non-empty probes; a simplified heuristic, not the
CertaIndex (CoT) rule of \S\ref{sec:exp-prior}) fires on $1{,}477/1{,}500$
trajectories and commits to answers correct only $50.5\%$ of the time, whereas
letting those same problems run to completion reaches $90.7\%$: a $40.2\pp$
loss. The cost is concentrated early: half of all consensuses form within the
first tenth of a trajectory's own length, and there the committed answer is
correct only $27.5\%$ of the time against $85.2\%$ for the same problems run to
completion (Figure~\ref{fig:consensus-pos}, appendix).

\subsection{Probe Wording versus Position}
\label{sec:mech-wording}
If early agreement reflected a settled belief, the answer a probe returns should
not depend much on how the probe is worded. We test this directly on paired
re-probes of the \emph{same frozen prefixes}: at every probe position we read the
trajectory with two different probe wordings (our boxed-answer suffix and the
CertaIndex suffix, both capped at $32$ tokens), so the model's state is identical
and only the elicitation differs.

  \centering

  \caption{\textbf{Two sides of the stability--terminality gap.}
  \textbf{(a)} The same frozen prefix (\dsseven{}, \textsc{MATH}500) re-probed
  with two differently worded answer queries. Early in a trajectory the two
  wordings return the \emph{same} current answer less than half the time ($43\%$
  in the first tenth) and the probed answer is usually wrong; by the end they
  nearly always coincide ($89\%$) and are usually correct. Position is a
  fraction of each trajectory's own length.
  \textbf{(b)} All $134$ stopped-but-wrong cases, labelled independently by two
  annotators ($\kappa{=}0.82$): three in five commit to an answer the model had
  \emph{not converged} on, one in six to a probe-format artifact, fewer than one
  in five to a genuinely settled wrong value.}
  \label{fig:wording-taxonomy}
\end{figure*}

Figure~\ref{fig:wording-taxonomy}(a) shows the result. In the first tenth of a
trajectory the two wordings return different answers $57\%$ of the time; by the
final third they disagree only $11\%$ of the time. Early ``answers'' are
therefore substantially a property of the question asked rather than of the
state being read, and they become properties of the state only as the
trajectory finishes.

This distinguishes our account from the obvious alternative. If the model simply
held a stable but mistaken belief early on, both wordings would read back the
\emph{same} wrong value; instead they disagree. What a consensus rule treats as a
converging answer is, early on, an output the probe has elicited. Note also that
probe wording has almost no effect on \emph{final} accuracy in the same paired
data (a $0.65\pp$ readout effect against a $9.15\pp$ effect of \emph{when} one
probes), so the sensitivity above is specific to early positions and is not a
defect of any particular suffix.

\subsection{Error Taxonomy}
\label{sec:mech-placeholder}
Two annotators independently labelled all $134$ stopped-but-wrong cases
(Figure~\ref{fig:wording-taxonomy}(b), $\kappa = 0.82$): only one in five is a case where
the model had genuinely settled on a wrong value. Three in five are answers it
had not converged on at all, and a further one in six are artifacts of the
probe's output format.

The reason is that a probe does not read the model's belief; it \emph{forces} an
answer. A model that has not finished reasoning must still write something in the
box, and the cheapest thing to write is what it wrote last time. Repetition of a
forced answer is therefore stable for reasons that have nothing to do with the
reasoning being finished, and a consensus rule cannot tell that case apart from a
settled one. This also predicts the window effect of \S\ref{sec:mech-ratio}: a
longer window is a longer buffer in which a placeholder can be overturned, which
is why it reduces false consensus without removing the underlying risk.

\subsection{Effect of Window Size}
\label{sec:mech-ratio}
The natural remedy is to demand more agreement before stopping---a stop only
when the last $W$ probes agree, for larger $W$. It helps, but only by stopping
less often. Consider only the stops where committing early \emph{changes}
correctness relative to the trajectory's own final answer, and count two
outcomes: (final-correct, stop-wrong)---a correct recovery destroyed---versus
(final-wrong, stop-correct)---a wrong answer luckily banked. Pure sampling
noise predicts a $\approx1{:}1$ ratio; a genuine ``continued reasoning
corrects'' effect predicts $\gg1$. On the frozen replay this ratio depends
strongly on the window: it falls from $\approx\!45{:}1$ for a latest-probe stop
($W{=}1$) to $\approx\!2{:}1$ at the largest window ($W{=}30$). But the low end
is bought only by stopping so rarely that net saving vanishes ($W{=}30$ fires
on $121$ dev problems vs.\ $668$ at $W{=}1$); at any window that actually saves
tokens the ratio stays well above $1{:}1$. DEER (the non-consensus control of
\S\ref{sec:exp-deer}), by contrast, holds $\approx\!2.4$--$3.5{:}1$ at its
three operating points \emph{while} saving $28$--$32\%$, because its
boundary-confidence signal commits mainly once the trajectory has settled.
Stopping on consensus therefore removes a correct-in-the-end answer many times
more often than it rescues a wrong one---directional, not sampling noise; the
recovery phenomenon of \S\ref{sec:exp-fc}, now as a signed effect.
Figure~\ref{fig:harm-rescue} plots the whole window axis, so the claim rests on
the shape of the curve rather than on either endpoint: the ratio and the saving
fall together, and DEER sits at consensus's best ratio and its best saving
simultaneously, which no window does.

Why does the ratio stay above one wherever the rule still fires? The recovery
it counts is a property of the reasoning trajectory: for a stop at token
position $s$, the correction forgone is whatever the trajectory would have made
after $s$, which depends on the trajectory, not on how it was probed. The
\emph{realized} accuracy cost of a rule is not itself
probe-independent---it depends on where the rule stops, and hence on the probe
schedule and stopping rule---but the recovery asymmetry that produces it is
present at every position where reasoning is still moving.
Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-cases} shows all three regimes on
individual probe streams, including a first-probe placeholder held for $27$
consecutive probes---long enough to outlast every window we searched.

\subsection{The Role of Independence}
\label{sec:mech-independence}
A structural fact underlies why a bad stop is never caught. Self-consistency's
majority vote can absorb an error because its paths are sampled independently
(\S\ref{sec:related}): a wrong answer on one path is outvoted by others that
reasoned differently. Probing one trajectory yields no such thing---every probe
reads the same prefix, so the readings move together, and when the prefix is wrong
they are wrong together. Agreement across probes is thus a statement about the
stability of one line of reasoning, not corroboration from several, so a consensus
rule holds no vote in reserve to overturn the placeholder it commits to.
Independence explains why the gap is \emph{dangerous} rather than merely present;
it does not on its own show the committed answer is non-terminal---that is what the
direct measurements above establish.

Whether some setting of the consensus signal nonetheless escapes the gap is an
empirical question; the next section closes it.
